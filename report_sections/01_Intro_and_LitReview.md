# 1. Introduction
1.1 Purpose of the Document
This document is the final technical report for the PortaPill automated medication dispensing system, developed over two semesters as the Electronic Systems Engineering capstone project at Conestoga College. It is the single consolidated record of the complete project lifecycle: the identification and justification of the problem, the review of existing solutions, the derivation of formal requirements, the engineering design of every functional unit, the physical realisation and testing of the prototype, the measured performance of the delivered system, and an assessment of how well that system met the goals set at the outset.
The document serves three distinct audiences:
●	Project evaluators and faculty, who require evidence that the work constitutes a substantial fourth-year engineering design effort founded on sound investigation, analysis and verification.
●	Technical and professional readers outside the project, including potential sponsors, clinicians and industry reviewers, who require an accurate account of what the system does, what it has been demonstrated to do, and what it has not.
This report supersedes and consolidates the four earlier project documents: the Capstone Project Proposal, the User Requirements and Project Plan, the Engineering Design Document and Test Plan, and the two Capstone II technical assessments. Where the delivered system differs from what was described in those earlier documents, this report describes the system as built and as measured, and Section 1.5 explicitly records the reasoning behind each significant change.
1.2 Project Definition
PortaPill is a portable, three-compartment automated medication dispensing system that stores three medications of differing pill geometry, dispenses the correct dose at scheduled times without user intervention, independently verifies through physical sensing that each pill was actually delivered, confirms that the user physically collected the dose, and alerts through visual, audible and where a network is available, push notification channels. Every function required to deliver medication safely executes on the device itself: no cloud service, no subscription, and no internet connection is required for scheduling, dispensing, verification or on-device alerting.
The system is an embedded electromechanical product integrating four engineering domains:
●	Mechanical: a custom 3D-printed enclosure and a rotating-disk pill singulation mechanism, designed parametrically in SolidWorks and iterated across multiple physical revisions.
●	Electronic hardware: power generation, regulation and uninterruptible battery backup; motor drive; sensor signal conditioning and GPIO protection; and a custom two-layer printed circuit board.
●	Embedded firmware: a real-time, interrupt-driven control application in C on the RP2350, and a concurrent FreeRTOS-based interface and networking application on the ESP32-S3.
●	Human-machine interface: an on-device graphical display with physical navigation controls, multi-modal alerting, and a locally hosted web dashboard.
1.3 Background
1.3.1 Problem / Need Statement
Medication non-adherence, patients not taking prescribed medication correctly, at the right time, or at all, is one of the most persistent and costly failures in modern healthcare delivery. The problem is concentrated among elderly patients, individuals managing chronic conditions, and anyone maintaining several concurrent prescriptions, precisely the populations for whom the consequences of a missed or doubled dose are most severe.
The failure is not a single problem but three distinct and compounding ones:
A physical accessibility barrier. Standard child-resistant “push-and-turn” pharmacy containers are, by design, difficult to open. For an elderly user with arthritis, reduced grip strength, or limited manual dexterity, this design intent becomes an exclusionary barrier. Users in this position routinely require assistance to access their own medication, which erodes independence and introduces a dependency on caregiver availability at dose time.
A cognitive and procedural load. Manual pill organisers, the most common intervention, transfer the entire burden of correctness onto the patient's memory and attention. The patient must remember to fill the organiser correctly, remember that a dose is due, and remember whether they have already taken it. Each of these is a failure point, and the third is particularly dangerous: uncertainty about whether a dose was taken leads either to a missed dose or to an accidental double dose, and the patient has no reliable way to resolve that uncertainty after the fact.
An absence of delivery verification. Almost no consumer device closes the loop. A dispenser that actuates a motor at the scheduled time and assumes success will silently fail when a pill jams, bridges, or fails to clear the dispensing path. The patient believes a dose was delivered; the log says a dose was delivered; no dose was delivered. In a medication-adherence device, an undetected failure is more dangerous than an obvious one, because it removes the patient's opportunity to intervene.
Existing automated solutions address the first two problems to varying degrees but introduce their own barriers: high acquisition cost, recurring subscription fees, mandatory continuous internet connectivity, complex touchscreen interfaces poorly suited to the target demographic, and large stationary form factors that eliminate portability. Section 2 examines these solutions in detail.
1.3.2 Rationale
Solving this problem is justified on four grounds.
The clinical benefit is evidenced, not speculative. The literature reviewed in Section 2 reports that automated dispensing interventions produce measurable, statistically meaningful improvements in adherence across several distinct patient populations: adherence rising from 91.17% to 98.35% in a randomised controlled trial of patients with chronic conditions [2], and from 49% to 96.8% over six months among elderly patients with memory disorders [3]. These are not marginal gains. A device in this category, if it works reliably, has a demonstrated capacity to change patient outcomes.
The accessibility benefit addresses a population that existing solutions underserve. By removing the requirement to physically open a child-resistant container, and by replacing a complex touch interface with large-format on-screen text, discrete physical buttons with tactile feedback, colour-coded status indication and an audible alert, PortaPill is designed for the user who is least well served by current consumer devices: an elderly patient with reduced dexterity and limited technical literacy. Accessibility here is not an added feature; it is the primary design constraint from which the interface decisions follow.
The economic benefit is a direct consequence of the architectural decision to operate offline. Market-leading prescription-based dispensers carry acquisition costs in the range of thousands of dollars and commonly require an ongoing monthly subscription for the monitoring and reminder services that constitute their core value. Because PortaPill performs scheduling, dispensing, verification, alerting and status reporting entirely on-device, there is no recurring server cost to pass on to the user, and therefore no structural reason for a subscription to exist. The bill of materials for the prototype is documented in Appendix D and is an order of magnitude below the retail cost of comparable prescription-based commercial units.
The safety benefit is the closed verification loop, and it is the project's principal engineering contribution. PortaPill does not assume a dispense succeeded because a motor was commanded to turn. Every dispense event must be independently confirmed by two physically distinct sensing modalities, a piezoelectric impact sensor detecting the pill striking the collection drawer and an infrared beam-break sensor confirming the pill physically transited the dispensing path, before the event is logged as successful and the pill count is decremented. If confirmation is not received, the system reverses the mechanism to clear a potential obstruction and retries; if it still cannot confirm delivery after three attempts, it declares an explicit failure to the user, illuminates the corresponding compartment indicator red, and does not decrement the pill count. The design principle is that the system should never silently claim success. This capability is largely absent from the consumer devices surveyed in Section 2, and it is the feature most directly aimed at the third failure mode identified in Section 1.3.1.
1.4 Proposed Solution
1.4.1 Description
PortaPill is a benchtop-scale, portable automated dispenser holding three independent medication compartments, each with its own storage container, dispensing mechanism, stepper motor, and dedicated verification sensor pair. Medication profiles (name, total pill count, pills per dose, and up to four scheduled dose times per day) are configured either through the on-device LCD interface using the physical button controls, or through a web dashboard served by the device's own Wi-Fi access point and reachable from any phone or laptop browser without an internet connection.
Once configured, the device operates autonomously. It evaluates its schedule against a software real-time clock, and at a scheduled dose time it actuates the stepper motor for the relevant compartment, singulates and releases the programmed number of pills, verifies each delivery through the dual-sensor sequence, decrements the pill count, logs the event, illuminates the compartment's RGB indicator green, sounds an audible alert, and updates both the LCD and the web dashboard. The dispensed dose lands in a sliding front drawer from which the user retrieves it, with no container to open and no cap to turn.
When a dispense cannot be verified, the system enters its recovery path rather than failing silently: it reverses the motor briefly to clear a potential obstruction, resets the sensor state, and retries, up to three attempts. Persistent failure produces a red indicator, an explicit on-screen error, and a preserved pill count so that the discrepancy is visible rather than buried.
Power is delivered through a HW-465A UPS module that maintains a regulated 5 V rail from either a USB-C wall supply or an internal 1S2P 18650 lithium-ion pack, switching between them without interruption. Both the RP2350 and the ESP32-S3 are powered directly from this 5 V rail. A separate LM317 linear regulator derives a dedicated 3.3 V rail solely for the sensor divider network (the IR, piezoelectric and Hall-effect circuits), isolating that sensitive analogue signal path from motor-induced transients on the 5 V rail.

1.4.2 Comparison to Existing Solutions
A detailed treatment appears in Section 2. In summary, PortaPill is differentiated from the surveyed field by the combination, not any single element, of four characteristics:
1.	Mandatory closed-loop dispense verification using two independent physical sensing modalities, with automatic retry and explicit failure reporting.
2.	Fully offline core operation. Scheduling, dispensing, verification, alerting and status reporting require no internet connection and no external service. The Wi-Fi interface is a locally hosted access point, not a cloud client.
3.	No subscription and no recurring cost, as a structural consequence of (2).
4.	An accessibility-first interface using physical buttons with tactile feedback, large-format display text, per-compartment colour-coded status, and audible alerting, rather than a dense touchscreen.
1.4.3 System Focus
The whole system. Considered as a complete product in its intended clinical context, an automated medication management system spans a chain that begins at the prescriber, passes through the pharmacy, reaches the patient's home, and returns as adherence data to the care team. That full chain comprises: (a) prescription generation and clinical review; (b) pharmacy dispensing, labelling and, in a mature implementation, machine-readable encoding of the prescription onto the medication container; (c) the in-home dispensing device that stores medication, executes the schedule, and physically delivers doses; (d) the patient interaction layer that alerts, confirms and reports; (e) an optional caregiver and clinician notification path; and (f) a refill and resupply loop.
Where this project focuses. PortaPill addresses stages (c) and (d), the in-home dispensing device and the patient interaction layer, and treats these as the complete scope of Version 1. Specifically, the engineering effort of this project is concentrated on:
●	the electromechanical dispensing and singulation mechanism that reliably releases a discrete, countable number of pills;
●	the closed-loop verification subsystem that physically confirms delivery rather than assuming it;
●	the real-time embedded control architecture that partitions time-critical dispensing from user-facing interaction across two microcontrollers;
●	the power management subsystem that guarantees the device continues to operate, and does not lose its schedule, through a mains power failure;
●	the accessibility-oriented user interface across display, physical controls, indicators, audio and web.
Explicitly outside this project's scope. Stage (b), pharmacist-side programming of the device via a machine-readable smart cap, was proposed in Capstone I and formally deferred; the reasoning is recorded in Section 1.5. Stage (e), remote caregiver notification, and stage (f), automated refill, are identified as future work in Section 14.5. This report does not claim these capabilities.

Each change was a deliberate engineering decision made in response to test evidence, scope reality, or a better design option identified during prototyping. They are recorded here in full.

1.5.1 The Third Verification Stage: Pickup Confirmation
The re-tasking of the Hall-effect sensor (change #3 above) produced a capability that was not part of the original design intent and is worth stating explicitly, because it extends the project's central contribution.
The verification argument of Section 1.3.1 identified that consumer dispensers cannot detect a failed delivery. PortaPill's dual-sensor design closes that gap, but only as far as the collection drawer. A dose correctly dispensed into a drawer that the patient never opens is, clinically, still a missed dose, and a device that logs it as a success has the same blind spot one stage further along.
The drawer sensor closes that remaining stage. The system therefore verifies medication delivery in three sequential stages:
Stage	Question answered	Sensing modality
1: Transit	Did a pill leave the mechanism and traverse the dispensing path?	IR beam-break
2: Delivery	Did it physically arrive in the collection drawer?	Piezoelectric impact
3: Retrieval	Did the patient actually collect it?	Hall effect, drawer open

Stage 3 drives the escalating pickup reminder sequence described in Section 5.5.3: a dose that is dispensed but not collected triggers reminders at four increasing intervals, each cancelled automatically the moment drawer opening confirms retrieval. Pickup state is tracked independently per station, so a collection at one compartment cannot mask an uncollected dose at another.
The three stages are logically and electrically independent: the piezoelectric and IR sensors confirm that a pill was dispensed, while the Hall-effect sensor confirms, separately, that the patient opened the drawer to retrieve it. A dispense is never inferred from a pickup event, and a pickup is never inferred from a dispense event. Verification and validation of each stage, including the Hall-effect sensor's response under drawer motion, is reported in Section 9.

 



# 2. Literature Review

2.1 Clinical Evidence for Automated Dispensing Devices
Automated medication dispensers are an active area of applied research, and the clinical literature establishes both the scale of the underlying problem and the measurable effect of dispensing interventions.
Gargioni, Fogli and Baroni [1], in a systematic review of pill and medication dispensers conducted from a human-centred perspective, characterise medication adherence as “a critical challenge in healthcare” and identify dispensers as an area of sustained attention as a means of promoting adherence and improving outcomes. The review reports adherence rates of approximately 98% and usability ratings of 85.7% among elderly users of commercial dispensers, with individual non-adherence in some deployments falling from above 25% to 11.9%. The framing of this review is particularly relevant to the present project: it evaluates dispensers on human-centred criteria rather than purely technical ones, which supports the accessibility-first design position taken in Section 1.4.
A pilot randomised controlled trial conducted in Calgary, Canada [2] provides controlled evidence of effect. Over a six-month observation period, patients living with chronic conditions who used an in-home electronic medication dispensing system achieved a mean adherence rate of 98.35%, against 91.17% in a control group using conventional non-technological methods. Because this is a randomised design rather than an observational study, it supports a causal reading of the dispenser's contribution, and it establishes that the benefit extends beyond home consumer use into rehabilitation and supervised care settings where the consequences of a missed dose are amplified.
Hoffmann et al. [3] examined adherence among elderly patients with diagnosed memory disorders, the population for which the cognitive-load failure mode described in Section 1.3.1 is most acute. Adherence in this cohort rose from 49% to 96.8% over six months of automated dispenser use. The magnitude of this improvement, in the population least able to compensate through memory alone, is the single strongest argument in the reviewed literature for automated dispensing as an assistive intervention.
Taken together, these sources establish three things relevant to this project: the problem is real and quantified; automated dispensing measurably improves adherence across several distinct populations; and the field remains an active subject of research and development rather than a solved problem.
The literature also establishes that automation reduces, but does not eliminate, dispensing error, and that a meaningful share of the errors that do occur are never caught before reaching the patient. An evaluation of carousel dispensing technology in a hospital pharmacy reported that overall dispensing accuracy rose from 99.02 percent to 99.48 percent after implementation [17], a real improvement, but one that leaves a small, non-zero error rate even under professional, continuously staffed conditions. Cina et al. [18] quantified what happens to that residual error: observing 140,755 pharmacist-filled doses, 3.6 percent contained an error, and routine pharmacist verification caught only 79 percent of them, meaning 0.75 percent of all doses dispensed carried an undetected error out of the pharmacy, of which 23.5 percent were potential adverse drug events. This is the strongest available evidence for the specific failure mode PortaPill targets: even in a hospital pharmacy, with trained staff and an established verification step, a measurable fraction of dispensing errors go undetected. An unattended home device with no human review step is not in a better position by default; it is in a worse one unless verification is built into the device itself. None of the consumer devices surveyed in Section 2.2 report any comparable verification step, a comparison developed further in Section 2.5.
2.2 Commercial Solutions
Commercial products divide into three tiers of increasing capability.
Tier 1: Timed and alarmed pill organisers. These do not dispense. A conventional multi-compartment organiser is paired with a programmable timer that alerts at dose time; the user still opens the compartment manually [4]. They are inexpensive and simple, but retain both failure modes identified in Section 1.3.1: the user must still physically open a container, and the device has no knowledge of whether a dose was taken.
Tier 2: Pre-dosed rotary dispensers. LiveFine [5] and MedReady [6] use a rotating disk of compartments, each pre-filled by the user or a caregiver with one dose. At the scheduled time the device rotates the disk to present the appropriate compartment, and can be locked to prevent inappropriate access. Capacity is typically up to 28 doses before refilling. Pria [7] extends this architecture with an integrated display, a virtual assistant, and messaging to both patient and caregiver. The critical limitation of this tier is that correctness has been displaced rather than removed: the device faithfully presents whatever was loaded into each compartment, so a loading error propagates silently to the patient. The device cannot detect it, because it has no knowledge of what a compartment should contain.
Tier 3: Prescription-based multi-container dispensers. MedaCube [9], [16] and Hero [8] hold each medication in a separate bulk container within the unit, are programmed with prescription information, and assemble and dispense the correct combination of medications at each scheduled time. This tier resolves the pre-loading error mode of Tier 2. Hero additionally supports as-needed medication with configurable per-hour and per-day limits to prevent excessive use [9]. These are the most capable devices surveyed and the closest functional comparators to PortaPill. Their limitations are commercial and architectural rather than functional: high acquisition cost, frequent subscription requirement for monitoring features, dependence on continuous connectivity, and large stationary form factors that preclude portability.
2.3 Proof-of-Concept and Open Development Work
Three non-commercial projects informed the mechanical design direction and are cited here as prior art rather than as authoritative technical references.
Tomasz's dispenser [10] uses stacked bowls of medication with a rotating disk releasing a single pill per rotation, with scheduling configured through a device-hosted web interface. The device-hosted web configuration approach (no cloud, no app, browser access to a locally served page) is architecturally the same position PortaPill adopts for its dashboard, and independently validates that choice. Its limitations are an increasing footprint as medication types are added, and an open construction with no tamper protection.
Joe Crop's Smart Desktop Medicine Organizer [11] is a more complete enclosed design built around a rotating drum of wells, controlled through an integrated touch display offering separate caregiver and patient interfaces. Like Pria, it requires that each dose be manually assembled into a well in advance, and so inherits the Tier 2 pre-loading limitation.
Michael Sangiorgio's Medi-Pal [12] demonstrates a purely mechanical mechanism for selectively dispensing individual pills from bulk storage, using three hoppers and feed mechanisms. The overall device is a limited proof of concept with no timing or quantity programming. However, the mechanical singulation principle it demonstrates, deterministic release of one pill at a time from a bulk container by geometry alone without sensing or active control, was the direct starting point for the PortaPill dispensing mechanism, as described in Section 2.6.
Additional academic and student work informed the design context, including a smart medical box for automatic pill dispensing and health monitoring [13] and the Pill Pal smart medication dispenser [14].
None of the proof-of-concept projects surveyed constitutes a complete end-to-end solution competitive with the commercial products in Section 2.2, and none implements verification of successful delivery.

2.5 How the Proposed Solution Differs
Every individual capability in PortaPill exists somewhere in the surveyed field. Bulk-storage dispensing exists in Tier 3. Offline device-hosted configuration exists in Tomasz's proof of concept. Battery backup exists in several consumer devices. The contribution of this project is not the invention of a new capability but the integration of a specific combination that no surveyed solution provides simultaneously, organised around a single design commitment: the device must never report a dose as delivered unless it has physically confirmed that it was.
Three differentiators follow from that commitment.
First, verification is mandatory and physical, not inferred. The surveyed devices establish success by construction: the motor was commanded, therefore the dose was delivered. PortaPill requires two independent physical confirmations from different sensing modalities before it will log success: a piezoelectric impact event confirming the pill struck the collection drawer, and an infrared beam-break confirming it transited the dispensing path. The two sensors were chosen precisely because neither can substitute for the other. The piezo detects arrival but cannot distinguish a pill from an incidental knock to the enclosure; the IR beam confirms transit through the correct path but cannot confirm the pill completed its fall. Requiring both, in sequence, within a bounded time window, discriminates a genuine dispense from either failure mode alone.
Second, failure is surfaced rather than absorbed. Because verification can fail, the system defines what happens when it does, reverse the mechanism to clear obstructions, reset sensor state, retry up to three times, and on persistent failure raise an explicit user-facing error while deliberately not decrementing the pill count. Preserving the count on failure means the device's record and the physical container remain consistent, so a subsequent manual reconciliation is possible. A device that decremented on a failed dispense would corrupt its own inventory silently.
Third, offline operation is architectural, not a fallback mode. The scheduling engine, dispense logic, three-stage verification, on-device alerting and profile persistence all execute on the device itself. The ESP32-S3 hosts its own Wi-Fi access point, so a phone or laptop connects directly to the device with no infrastructure between them. There is no account, no mandatory service, and no cloud outage that can prevent a patient from receiving medication.
The delivered system additionally supports joining an existing Wi-Fi network, which enables an optional remote push notification channel (Section 5.5.3). This is a deliberate layering rather than a full departure from the offline position: connectivity extends reach, allowing a caregiver to be informed but no core function depends on it. With the network removed, the device continues to dispense on schedule, verify delivery and retrieval, and alert locally through display, indicator and audible channels. The distinction is between a device that uses connectivity and one that requires it, and PortaPill is deliberately the former. Because push notification delivery necessarily involves a network path to an external notification service, the precise claim is stated as follows: all medication data required for operation is stored and processed on-device; no medication data is transmitted unless the user explicitly enables notifications; and no continuous telemetry, analytics, or third-party data sharing exist in any configuration. The content of the notification payload itself, and the privacy implications of that content, are addressed in Section 11.4.
