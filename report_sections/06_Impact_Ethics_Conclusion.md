10.3 Societal Impact
Positive impacts.
The system addresses a documented public health problem with evidenced clinical benefit (§2.1). Its principal societal contributions are:
●	Improved medication adherence, with the associated reduction in avoidable hospitalisation and disease progression that the literature attributes to dispensing interventions.
●	Support for independent living. Users who require assistance to open medication containers or to remember doses currently depend on caregiver availability at dose time. A device that removes both barriers extends the period during which such a person can live independently — an outcome with quality-of-life value beyond its clinical effect.
●	Reduced caregiver burden. Medication management is a recurring, time-bound daily task for family caregivers. Automating dose preparation and delivery reduces both the time cost and the anxiety of uncertainty about whether a dose was taken.
●	Cost accessibility. The absence of subscription requirements removes a recurring financial barrier that disproportionately affects fixed-income patients — precisely the elderly population most likely to need the device.
●	Privacy by architecture. Because no medication data leaves the device, users are not required to trade medical privacy for functionality. This is a substantive societal benefit at a time when health data commonly flows to third-party services as a condition of use.

10.4 Environmental Impact
Positive impacts and design measures.
●	Rechargeable rather than disposable energy storage. The 1S2P 18650 pack replaces what would otherwise be a continuing stream of primary cells over the device's service life, eliminating that waste entirely.
●	User-replaceable cells. Because the cells can be replaced when they degrade, cell end-of-life does not force disposal of the whole device. Service life is governed by the electronics and mechanism, not by the shortest-lived component.
●	Modular, repairable mechanical design. Components are individually printable and replaceable. A damaged mechanism, drawer or container is replaced individually rather than requiring a new unit. Print files can be reissued indefinitely, so spare-part availability is not limited by manufacturer support.
●	RoHS-compliant electronic components sourced through standard commercial channels, limiting hazardous substance content.
●	Recyclable and low-impact print material. PLA is derived from renewable feedstock and is industrially compostable; PETG is recyclable. Both avoid the hazardous additives associated with some engineering plastics.
●	Low operating energy. The device operates at a small fraction of the power of a typical household appliance, and its lifetime energy consumption is negligible.
Negative impacts and their mitigation.
Negative impact	Mitigation
Lithium-ion cells are an environmental hazard at end of life, containing heavy metals and organic electrolytes that must not enter landfill.	Cells are user-removable, so they can be separated for proper recycling rather than being disposed of embedded in the device. The criterion has two halves: (a) integrating standards, codes and regulatory factors into decision making, and (b) protection of the public. 11.1 Professional Ethical Framework
This project was conducted in the context of a device intended to handle prescription medication for vulnerable users. That context imposes obligations beyond ordinary engineering practice, and the design decisions recorded throughout this report were made with reference to the professional obligations expected of engineers in Ontario.
The Professional Engineers Ontario Code of Ethics establishes that a practitioner's duty is owed first to the public welfare, and requires that a practitioner act as a faithful agent, act with fairness and loyalty, and — critically for a student team — undertake only work they are competent to perform. Three principles governed decision making on this project:
The primacy of public welfare over project ambition. Where a design option offered greater capability at the cost of reduced safety assurance, the safer option was taken. The clearest instance is the decision to require dual-sensor verification for every dispense despite the resulting complexity, additional components, additional failure modes and additional development time. A simpler device that assumed successful dispensing would have been faster to build and easier to make appear reliable in a demonstration. It would also have been capable of silently failing to deliver a dose — and that possibility was judged unacceptable in a medication device regardless of its effect on project convenience.
Honest representation of capability. This report states what the system has been demonstrated to do and, equally, what it has not. Section 9.4 documents the limits of the evidence; Section 9.5 lists known limitations; Section 8.8 reports an unresolved anomaly. Overstating a medical-adjacent device's reliability is not merely an academic failing — if such a claim influenced someone's decision to rely on the device, it would carry real consequences. The device is described as a prototype throughout, because that is what it is.
Competence and its boundaries. The team recognised that certain aspects of a medication device — clinical validation, regulatory certification, pharmaceutical handling — lie outside both its competence and the scope of an academic project. Rather than approximating them, they are explicitly excluded and identified as prerequisites for any real-world deployment (§11.3).

11.3 Regulatory Context
A device that dispenses prescription medication to patients sits within a regulated space, and the boundary between this prototype and a deployable product is a regulatory one as much as a technical one. Stating that boundary accurately is a professional obligation.
Canadian regulatory position. Medical devices in Canada are regulated by Health Canada under the Medical Devices Regulations (SOR/98-282) of the Food and Drugs Act, which classify devices into Classes I through IV by risk. A device that automatically dispenses prescription medication on a schedule, where malfunction could result in a missed or incorrect dose, would attract classification and require a medical device licence, evidence of conformity to a recognised quality management system, and evidence of safety and effectiveness before it could be marketed.

11.4 Ethical Design Decisions
Several design decisions were made on ethical rather than technical grounds. They are recorded here because the rubric asks specifically about integrating these factors into decision making.
Verification was made mandatory rather than optional. There is no configuration in which the system will log a dispense as successful without both sensor confirmations. Making verification defeatable would have simplified demonstration and debugging considerably. It was rejected because a configurable safety feature is a safety feature that will eventually be switched off.
The pill count is not decremented on a failed dispense. This was a deliberate choice with a specific rationale: preserving the count keeps the system's inventory record consistent with the physical container, so a subsequent manual reconciliation is possible. Decrementing on failure would have produced a cleaner-looking log and a silently corrupted inventory.
Concurrent dispense requests are rejected rather than queued. The HTTP 409 response on a second dispense request during an active cycle prevents a double dose arising from a double-tap, a page refresh, or two people operating the dashboard simultaneously. Queueing would have been the more conventional software behaviour; in this application it would have been the more dangerous one.
Configuration authority is deliberately constrained. The system does not recommend, adjust or optimise medication schedules. It executes what a human configured. AI-based medication recommendation was explicitly placed out of scope (R-28) — not because it was infeasible, but because a device offering medication recommendations without clinical validation would be irresponsible regardless of how well it worked.
No data leaves the device. Cloud logging was placed out of scope (R-29) and the network interface is a local access point rather than a cloud client. Medication data is among the more sensitive categories of personal information, and the decision not to transmit it removes an entire class of privacy and breach risk rather than attempting to manage it.
Security posture and its recognised limits. The wireless link is encrypted under WPA2, and notification traffic is carried over HTTPS, so medication data is protected in transit on both paths. Two weaknesses remain, and are stated here rather than left to be discovered:
●	The access point passphrase is a weak shared default rather than a per-unit credential. Any person who has ever been told the passphrase, for any unit, can join any unit.
●	API endpoints are not individually authenticated. Network membership alone authorises a dispense command.
Neither is acceptable in a production medication device, and neither is difficult to fix: a per-unit passphrase generated at manufacture and printed on the enclosure, plus authentication on all state-changing endpoints. Both are specified in §14.5. They are recorded as prototype limitations, not as design positions.

It will also almost certainly be asked about in Q&A — and having addressed it in writing first puts you in a considerably better position than being asked about something you concealed.
11.5 Equity, Accessibility and Design Justice
The capstone guideline asks teams to consider the design justice of their work — who benefits, who is excluded, and whether the design reproduces or reduces existing inequities.
Who this design serves. The primary beneficiary is an elderly or chronically ill patient managing multiple medications, potentially with reduced dexterity, reduced vision, memory impairment, or limited technical literacy. This population is systematically underserved by consumer electronics designed around able-bodied, technically fluent users, and design decisions throughout this project were made in their favour, sometimes at a cost:
●	Physical buttons over touch — less input flexibility, more reliable actuation for a user with tremor.
●	Large-format display type — less information density, more legibility with impaired vision.
●	Colour-coded per-compartment indication — communicates outcome without requiring the display to be read at all.
●	Audible alerting — reaches a user who cannot see the display.
●	Offline operation — serves users without reliable home internet, who skew older, lower-income and rural.
●	No subscription — removes a recurring cost barrier that disproportionately excludes fixed-income patients.

The professional-verification gap identified in §1.3.1 as one of the three core problems is therefore not closed by Version 1. The smart cap concept was the intended solution and was deferred for capacity reasons. Its absence means the device improves adherence for users who have someone able to set it up, and does less for the most isolated users, who are also among the most in need. Stating this plainly matters more than claiming universal benefit.

13.3 Risk Management Reflection
The highest-scoring risk in the register was R-11, team capacity (RPN 160) — and it was the one that materialised. The team was reduced from three members to two after Capstone I, while the project scope had been defined for three. The response was progressive, deliberate scope reduction: the smart cap concept was formally deferred to "Won't" (R-14), several features were moved from "Must" to "Should" or "Could", and the core dispensing, verification and power functionality was protected as non-negotiable.
That decision is worth examining honestly. Deferring the smart cap removed the feature that addressed the professional verification gap — one of the three core problems identified in §1.3.1 — and its absence is the most significant limitation of the delivered system (§11.5). The alternative, however, was to attempt the full scope with reduced capacity, which would most likely have produced a system in which nothing worked reliably. Choosing to deliver a smaller system that works well over a larger system that works poorly was the correct engineering decision, and the risk register is what made it a decision rather than a drift.

14.5 Recommendations for Future Work
[RUBRIC: recommendations must be "adequately specific and justifiable." Each item below states what to do, why (traced to a specific limitation), and what it would take. Maintain that standard for anything you add.]
Priority 1 — Complete before this design is advanced further
1.1 Add a hardware real-time clock. Fit a DS3231 or equivalent I²C RTC module with coin-cell backup. Why: the software RTC loses time on total power failure, and a scheduling device that cannot keep time through a power interruption has a functional gap in its primary purpose (§9.5 #1, risk R-12, RPN 96). Cost: approximately $5, two GPIO for I²C, minor firmware change to read time at boot rather than requesting it over UART. This is the highest value-per-effort improvement available to the design.
1.2 Resolve the progressive current-draw anomaly. Establish the root cause per the diagnostic sequence in §8.9, most probably incomplete stepper coil de-energization on the dispense completion and error paths. Why: it degrades battery runtime, implies unintended power dissipation in a sealed enclosure containing lithium cells, and indicates a state not being correctly released in a safety-relevant system (§8.8, §9.5 #4). Cost: likely a firmware change only.
1.3 Harden the network interface. Replace the shared default access point passphrase with a device-unique key generated at manufacture and printed on the enclosure, and require authentication on all state-changing API endpoints (/api/profile, /api/dispense, /api/time-sync). Why: WPA2 encryption is already in place, but a shared default credential provides materially less protection than a per-unit key, and network membership alone should not authorise a dispense command (§11.4, §9.5 #2). Cost: low — a provisioning step and an endpoint guard, no new hardware.
1.4 Characterise dispensing with real pharmaceutical geometries. Test across a range of over-the-counter tablet and capsule sizes and shapes, recording success rate and failure mode per geometry, and publish the supported operating envelope. Why: risk R-04 (RPN 96, High) remains open, and reliability claims currently extend only to the non-medication analogues tested (§9.4.2). Cost: test time; possibly additional mechanism variants, which the modular design already supports.
1.5 Run an extended soak test. A minimum of 72 hours of continuous operation with scheduled dispensing, logging every event, current draw and any fault. Why: the longest test to date is 30 minutes, leaving thermal drift, clock drift, memory behaviour and cumulative mechanical wear uncharacterised for a device intended to run for months (§9.4.2). Cost: unattended time.
Priority 2 — Next development cycle
2.1 Implement the smart cap. A machine-readable medication container cap — NFC or contact EEPROM — carrying prescription data, with a pharmacy-side programming interface. Why: this closes the professional verification gap identified in §1.3.1 and left unaddressed by Version 1, and it is the feature that most directly serves the isolated users currently least well served (§11.5). It is also the project's original distinguishing concept. Cost: substantial — cap electronics, reader hardware, protocol, pharmacy-side tooling. Suitable as a capstone project in its own right.
2.2 Extend notification to a designated caregiver recipient. The notification and escalation infrastructure is built and verified — push delivery, four-stage escalation on uncollected doses, and per-station independence (§5.5.3). The remaining work is recipient management: allowing a caregiver, distinct from the patient, to be registered as an additional or alternative recipient. Why: escalation currently reaches the patient, who in the cognitively impaired case may be exactly the person unable to act on it; routing an escalated reminder to a second person closes that gap (§11.5). Cost: small relative to the work already done — recipient configuration and a routing rule, not new infrastructure. This is now the highest value-per-effort feature remaining, because the difficult part is already finished.
2.3 Replace the LM317 with a switching regulator. Why: the LM317 dissipates (5.0 − 3.3) × I_3V3 continuously as heat, directly reducing battery runtime against R-19 (§5.1.4, §8.7). Trade-off to manage: the linear regulator was chosen deliberately for its low output noise on the sensor supply rail (§5.1.2). A switching regulator would require careful output filtering and re-verification of piezo signal integrity. Quantify the runtime gain before committing, and validate sensor performance after the change.
2.4 Gate the IR emitters. Power the IR transmitter pairs from a controlled line active only during dispense cycles. Why: at approximately 10 mA per pair across three slots, continuously energised emitters are a significant fraction of idle draw for a function needed only during a dispense (§8.9). Cost: one GPIO and a switching element per emitter, or a single shared control line.
2.5 Revise the button housing for improved tactile feedback. Recorded as an outstanding issue in functional testing. Why: insufficient pre-travel resistance means users cannot reliably tell whether a press registered without also watching the LCD — poor for a device intended for users with limited dexterity or vision (§8.5). Partially addressed by the 5-way switch; confirm and complete.
2.6 Scale to more compartments. Why: three compartments do not serve patients on more than three concurrent medications, and the control architecture already supports five profiles (§9.5 #8). Constraint: GPIO count and physical envelope; a GPIO expander or a second driver board would be required beyond a certain point.
Priority 3 — Toward a deployable product
3.1 Establish the regulatory path. Determine Health Canada classification, establish an ISO 13485 quality management system, apply ISO 14971 risk management, and document the software lifecycle per IEC 62304 (§11.3).
3.2 Obtain safety and EMC certification. Electrical safety certification through CSA or an equivalent recognised body, and electromagnetic compatibility testing (§11.3).
3.3 Conduct usability evaluation with representative users. Formal usability engineering per IEC 62366 with participants drawn from the actual target demographic — elderly users with varying dexterity, vision and technical literacy. Why: every accessibility decision in this design was made on behalf of that population without testing with it. Note: human-subject research requires Research Ethics Board approval before any participant involvement.
3.4 Design for manufacture. Transition from FDM printing to injection moulding; consolidate modules onto a single board; design for automated assembly and for disassembly at end of life (§10.4).
3.5 Add a physical lock. A lockable medication compartment. Why: currently unmitigated hazard H8 — the device stores accessible medication and does not lock, which is a genuine concern in households with children or vulnerable adults (§10.1). Explicitly out of scope for V1 (R-31).
